Most people leave an assisted living tour without asking a single question about staffing at night. They ask about the dining room, the activities calendar, the square footage of the apartment: the things a tour naturally puts in front of you. The questions that best predict whether a loved one will be safe tend not to come up on their own, partly because they’re the less pleasant things to raise, and partly because nobody thinks to ask them until they’ve been through this once.

A tour shows you a Tuesday at 11 a.m. Your loved one will also be living there on a Saturday at 11 p.m., and the distance between those two is where most of the problems I’ve seen actually live. It isn’t that anyone is hiding the second one; it’s that no visit puts it in front of you, so you have to ask.

The questions below are grouped by what each one tells you. You won’t get to all of them in a single visit, and you shouldn’t try. Pick the ones that fit your loved one’s situation. Pay as much attention to *how* a question gets answered as to the answer itself. A specific answer usually means someone has thought the problem through. A vague one isn’t proof of anything, but it’s worth noticing, and it’s fair to follow up.

## What is an assisted living tour actually for?

A tour is, among other things, a sales appointment, and there’s nothing wrong with that. Every community does it, the way any organization puts its best foot forward for a visitor. But it’s useful to name, because it tells you what a tour is good for and what it isn’t. The person showing you around usually works in marketing or community relations. They’re frequently warm, knowledgeable people who care about the place. They’re also, generally, not the people who will be on the floor at 2 a.m. when your loved one needs help, which is why some of your questions need to go past them.

So treat the visit as information-gathering rather than a verdict. You’re collecting things you can actually compare across communities, and filling in what a scheduled visit can’t show you on its own. Go at an off-peak time if you can (late afternoon, a weekend, around a shift change) and ask to see the parts of the building that aren’t on the usual route. A few unplanned minutes are often worth more than the whole scheduled tour.

## What should I ask about staffing?

In my experience, staffing predicts care quality more reliably than anything else you can ask about on a tour, and it’s the topic communities are most practiced at answering vaguely. Push past the vague answers.

**“What is your caregiver-to-resident ratio during the day, in the evening, and overnight?”** The daytime number is almost always fine. The overnight number is the one to write down. Many communities staff far more thinly between roughly 11 p.m. and 7 a.m.: the exact window when a disoriented older adult gets up, tries to reach the bathroom alone, and falls. If you’re given only a daytime ratio, ask the overnight question again, directly.

**“Who is the most clinically trained person in the building overnight?”** Many assisted living communities have no licensed nurse on site overnight at all; care is delivered by aides who phone a nurse or call 911 when something happens. That may be perfectly fine for your loved one’s needs. It may not be. You can’t weigh it if you don’t know it. Ask whether overnight staff are required to stay awake, too. In some places they’re permitted to sleep and respond to call lights when they wake.

**“What’s your staff turnover over the past year, and how long have the aides on this floor been here?”** Staffing churn in this industry is substantial, and the price of it lands on care rather than on the org chart. Catching a change early requires having something to compare it against. An aide who has worked the same floor for two years knows what your loved one’s ordinary morning looks like and registers the day it isn’t ordinary, which is most of what early detection consists of, particularly for someone with cognitive impairment who can’t report the change themselves. A floor staffed largely by people who met the residents last month has no such reference point. Low turnover also tends to mean staff are treated decently, and that shows up in care.

## What should I ask about how care changes over time?

Your loved one’s needs will change. These questions are about what happens when they do.

**“How do you assess level of care, and how often is it reassessed?”** Assisted living typically charges a base rent plus a care fee that scales with how much help a resident needs. Understand how they measure that, who decides, and how often it’s revisited, because a reassessment is also a rate increase, and the climbing bill is one of the most common financial surprises in senior living. ([What “Level of Care” Really Means](https://geriatriciansguide.com/levels-of-care) breaks down how that pricing escalates.)

**“At what point would my loved one no longer be able to stay here?”** Every community has a ceiling: a point where a resident’s needs exceed what it’s licensed or willing to provide, and the resident must move. Ask where that ceiling is, and ask specifically about the things you can see coming: advancing dementia, assistance with transferring out of bed, or hospice. A community that can’t support someone through a decline means a second move later, at the worst possible time.

**“If my loved one’s dementia progresses, can they move to memory care here, or would they have to leave?”** If your loved one has any cognitive impairment, this is essential. Some communities have a secured memory care wing and can move a resident internally; others discharge. The difference decides whether a future change means a new hallway or a new building. ([Memory Care vs. Assisted Living](https://geriatriciansguide.com/memory-care-vs-assisted-living) covers that distinction in depth.)

## What should I ask about medication management?

Medication management is the part of daily care you’ll have the least visibility into. Your loved one’s regimen will be handled every day by people you won’t meet on the tour, through a process nobody will walk you through unless you ask. Three points in that process account for most of what goes wrong: the daily handoff, the moment someone decides a resident needs something for agitation, and the return from a hospital stay.

**“Who administers medications, and what is their training?”** In many states, medications in assisted living are given by aides with a short certification, not by nurses. Ask what the process is, who supervises it, and what happens when a dose is missed or refused.

**“What do you do before putting a resident on a medication for agitation?”** Antipsychotics carry a boxed warning for increased mortality in older adults with dementia, and their overuse to manage behavior is a long-standing problem in this industry. What you want to hear is that behavior gets investigated first (pain, constipation, infection, an overstimulating environment), because agitation in someone who can’t express themselves is usually a signal of discomfort rather than a psychiatric symptom.

**“Who reconciles the medication list after a hospital stay, and how fast?”** Transitions are where I see the most medication errors: a discharge can bring new prescriptions, stopped ones, and changed doses all at once. A good community has a clear answer about who catches the changes and how an order makes it from the doctor into the daily routine.

## What should I ask about food and eating?

Dining gets plenty of attention on a tour, and almost none of the useful kind. You’ll see the dining room. You may be fed. The question to ask isn’t about the food. It’s about who’s watching.

**“Who would notice if my loved one started losing weight, and what happens next?”** Unintended weight loss is one of the earliest signs that something has gone wrong: a new illness, a swallowing problem, depression, a medication side effect, or simply that eating has become difficult to manage without help. It is also one of the easiest signals to catch and completely invisible if no one is tracking it. Listen for whether weighing residents is a defined routine with a name attached to it, or something that happens when somebody thinks of it. By the time weight loss is visible across a dining room, it has usually been underway for months.

## What should I ask about falls and emergencies?

**“Walk me through exactly what happens when a resident falls.”** Listen for specifics: who responds, how fast, who gets called, how the family is notified, whether and when a resident goes to the hospital. The quality of this answer tells you whether there’s a real protocol or improvisation.

**“How are call lights answered, and do you measure response times by shift?”** Most systems log this, so a community that manages it can give you the number, and one that can’t produce it is answering you either way. Then ask the question behind the question: what happens for a resident who can’t reliably use the button? A call system assumes the person can recognize they need help and act on it, which is among the first abilities dementia takes. For those residents the protection isn’t a faster response time. It’s how often someone lays eyes on them without being summoned.

**“What’s your protocol for a resident who wanders or tries to leave?”** Relevant for anyone with cognitive impairment. A community that’s properly equipped for this will describe a specific process: what triggers a response, who checks, what the doors do.

## What should I ask about cost and the contract?

**“What’s the full monthly cost for someone with my loved one’s specific needs — base rent plus every care fee?”** Get a real number for *your* situation, not the brochure’s starting price. The national average for assisted living is about $6,200 a month as of 2026, and the figure on the marketing sheet is almost always the floor.

**“What’s the community fee, is any of it refundable, and what does it cover?”** Most communities charge a one-time move-in fee of several thousand dollars. Know the amount and the terms before you fall for the place.

**“Under what circumstances can you ask a resident to leave, and how much notice do I get?”** This lives in the contract, and it’s among the clauses people most regret skipping. Ask for a blank copy to take home. Don’t sign anything on the day of the tour. ([How to Read an Assisted Living Contract](https://geriatriciansguide.com/assisted-living-contract) walks through the common clauses that matter.)

## The question almost no one asks: “Where else should I be looking?”

Before you commit to assisted living at all, ask whether it’s even the right level. A small **residential care home** (also called a board-and-care or adult family home, a regular house licensed for a handful of residents) often delivers more hands-on attention than a large community, at a comparable or lower price, precisely because the staff-to-resident ratio is small. These homes have less to show on a tour: no chandelier, no bistro. That’s exactly why they get overlooked. For someone who needs close, consistent attention more than amenities, one of these can be the better answer. Tour at least one alongside the larger communities.

## What should I look for that no one will point out?

Some of the most useful information on a tour isn’t spoken. As you walk:

- **The smell.** Not food, not cleaning product: the ambient smell of the space. One bad moment means nothing; every building has them. Odor that has settled into a hallway is a different thing, and what it usually reflects is how many hands are available to manage continence care on that shift.
- **The residents.** Are people up, dressed, and engaged, or parked in front of a hallway television? A whole common room of disengaged residents is different from a few people resting.
- **The corners off the route.** Ask for an apartment someone could move into next month rather than the furnished showpiece, and ask to walk a hallway or use a bathroom the route doesn’t include. Lobbies get attention everywhere. The standard a building keeps where nobody is expected to look is the one your loved one will live inside.

## What should I do after the tour?

Before you leave, ask for two things: the names of the executive director and the director of nursing, and the community’s most recent state licensing or inspection report.

If they don’t hand the report over, you can find it yourself, though not where most people look first. Assisted living is licensed by the state rather than certified by Medicare, so it doesn’t appear on the federal [Care Compare](https://www.medicare.gov/care-compare/) site the way nursing homes do. Your state’s licensing agency, usually housed under Social Services, Health, or Aging, is where that record lives, and in most states it’s public.

Then read it at home, away from the building. This decision is difficult to reverse, and it deserves more than the momentum of a visit that went well.
